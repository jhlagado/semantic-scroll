# Machines, Intelligence and Subjectivity

By John Hardy

I’ve been following some of the talk on social media about whether we’ve cracked intelligence. There’s plenty of excitement about AGI, or artificial general intelligence. What bothers me is how easily that becomes a conversation about consciousness, as though solving one problem would settle the other. I think we have a form of automated intelligence. These systems can solve problems and turn up useful connections that people have overlooked. Calling it pattern matching doesn’t make that achievement disappear. But I don’t see why acknowledging it should commit me to believing that a computer experiences anything. I’ve always been sceptical of AI, particularly the claims about minds, and I haven’t seen a good reason to change that view. Underneath the fluent conversation there are matrices and calculations of what token should come next: next-word guessing, roughly speaking, on an enormous scale. The results can be remarkable. What I don’t see is how we get from that to a subject having an experience.

The term “neural network” makes the comparison with a brain sound closer than I think we’re entitled to assume. An artificial neuron is a mathematical simplification. Adding more of them, or developing better ways to train them through backpropagation, doesn’t by itself establish that we’ve reproduced how a brain works. We can explain particular mechanisms in neurons, such as [electrical signalling and chemical transmission at synapses](https://www.ninds.nih.gov/sites/default/files/2025-05/know-your-brain-brian-basics.pdf). How complete an understanding does that give us? I’m sceptical of the jump from explaining those mechanisms to claiming we understand the brain. Researchers do use artificial networks to [model aspects of it](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-11786-6), but those models are partial. Calling the components neurons doesn’t establish that they work like the real thing, much less explain subjective experience.

I’m still strongly sympathetic to Roger Penrose’s position. In *The Emperor’s New Mind* and *Shadows of the Mind*, he argues that human understanding cannot be fully captured by computation. His [argument for non-computability](https://arxiv.org/html/1205.5823) is disputed, but I don’t think impressive output from an LLM answers it. I’m not claiming he’s been proved right, but I haven’t found the case for abandoning his position convincing. Hofstadter’s idea of a self arising through [strange loops](https://www.wired.com/2007/03/me-my-soul-and-i/) raises a related question. A model can generate sentences about itself. Whether that amounts to the kind of self-model he describes, let alone an experience of being that self, is something else again.

I can also take seriously the argument that we might never know whether a machine has an experience of its own. But if we don’t know, then we don’t know. That uncertainty doesn’t establish consciousness, or rule it out. It certainly doesn’t justify assuming we’ve achieved it because the conversation sounds convincing. I’m happy to recognise useful machine intelligence. I remain sceptical that there’s anyone in there experiencing it. So far, the better answers have changed my view of what these tools can do. They haven’t changed my view on that.
