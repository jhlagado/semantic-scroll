# Machines, Intelligence and Subjectivity

By John Hardy

I’ve been following the talk on social media about whether we’ve cracked intelligence, with all the excitement about AGI, or artificial general intelligence. I think there are reasonable grounds to say we have a form of automated intelligence already: these systems can solve problems and turn up useful connections that people have overlooked, which is quite an achievement for pattern matching. What bothers me is how easily the conversation moves from those capabilities to consciousness, as though solving problems also establishes that a computer has a subjective experience of its own.

Underneath the fluent conversation there are matrices and calculations of what token should come next: next-word guessing, roughly speaking, on an enormous scale. The term “neural network” encourages a comparison with the brain that I think we take too far, given that an artificial neuron is a mathematical simplification. Adding more of them and improving their training through backpropagation still leaves us with the question of how closely any of this resembles the biology. We can explain particular mechanisms in real neurons, including [electrical signalling and chemical transmission at synapses](https://www.ninds.nih.gov/sites/default/files/2025-05/know-your-brain-brian-basics.pdf), and researchers use artificial networks to [model aspects of the brain](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-11786-6). Those models are partial, and I’m sceptical of how much we can infer from their success about the workings of a brain, especially when the claim extends to subjective experience.

Roger Penrose, the mathematician and physicist, set out his case against a computational account of the mind in *The Emperor’s New Mind*. His [argument draws on Gödel’s incompleteness theorems](https://arxiv.org/html/1205.5823): he argues that mathematical understanding allows us to recognise truths beyond the reach of a formal system whose rules we accept as sound. From this he concludes that human understanding cannot be fully captured by an algorithm. My position is that Penrose is right, and I regard his reasoning as rigorous. Producing impressive answers with a language model doesn’t address the limits he is arguing for, however useful those answers happen to be.

Penrose encountered opposition from researchers in AI and the philosophy of mind long before the current excitement about LLMs. In a [1996 reply to his critics](https://calculemus.org/MathUniversalis/NS/10/01penrose.html), he answered people including AI researcher John McCarthy and philosopher David Chalmers, and objected that some critics approached his argument assuming it must contain a flaw. The disagreement concerns whether the right computation would itself be enough to produce a mind. Chalmers [defends that position explicitly](https://consc.net/papers/computation.html), arguing that reproducing the relevant causal organisation would reproduce mental properties. That is a much stronger claim than saying computers can help us explain the brain, and it is the claim I reject.

I see motivated reasoning in the insistence that this further step must be possible. For people committed to a computational account of the mind, Penrose challenges a basic part of their understanding of the world; for the tech industry, the prospect of artificial minds is an enormously attractive story to sell. A living brain belongs to an organism whose continued existence depends on what it does. A computer model can represent survival, but representing those conditions doesn’t establish that there is a subject with anything at stake. That jump from modelling a living thing to creating its subjective experience is exactly where I want the explanation, and the industry’s enthusiasm gives me no reason to accept it.

I can take seriously the argument that we might never be able to know whether a machine has an experience of its own, and we might have to live with that uncertainty however capable these systems become. That uncertainty allows for possibilities in both directions; it gives us no grounds to assume consciousness has arrived. I think anyone making that claim needs to explain how they’ve established it from the behaviour we can actually observe, because a fluent conversation is precisely what these systems have been trained to produce.
