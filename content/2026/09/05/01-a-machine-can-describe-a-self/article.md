# Machines, Intelligence and Subjectivity

By John Hardy

I’ve been following the talk on social media about whether we’ve cracked intelligence, with all the excitement about AGI, or artificial general intelligence. I think there are reasonable grounds to say we have a form of automated intelligence already: these systems can solve problems and turn up useful connections that people have overlooked, which is quite an achievement for pattern matching. What bothers me is how easily the conversation moves from those capabilities to consciousness, as though solving problems also establishes that a computer has a subjective experience of its own.

Underneath the fluent conversation there are matrices and calculations of what token should come next: next-word guessing, roughly speaking, on an enormous scale. The term “neural network” encourages a comparison with the brain that I think we take too far, given that an artificial neuron is a mathematical simplification. Adding more of them and improving their training through backpropagation still leaves us with the question of how closely any of this resembles the biology. We can explain particular mechanisms in real neurons, including [electrical signalling and chemical transmission at synapses](https://www.ninds.nih.gov/sites/default/files/2025-05/know-your-brain-brian-basics.pdf), and researchers use artificial networks to [model aspects of the brain](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-019-11786-6). Those models are partial, and I’m sceptical of how much we can infer from their success about the workings of a brain, especially when the claim extends to subjective experience.

My position is that Roger Penrose is right: human understanding cannot be fully captured by computation. I regard the [argument he develops](https://arxiv.org/html/1205.5823) in *The Emperor’s New Mind* as rigorous, and impressive output from an LLM doesn’t answer it. Much of the dismissal strikes me as motivated reasoning from people who already expect a mind to emerge from enough computation. I’ve always been sceptical of that expectation, and dressing it up with the latest model’s performance gives me no reason to change my view. If the claim is that Penrose has been refuted, I want to see where his argument fails; enthusiasm for the technology won’t do that work. Douglas Hofstadter’s account of a self arising through [strange loops](https://www.wired.com/2007/03/me-my-soul-and-i/) leaves me with a similar question about what these models are doing. A model can generate sentences about itself, but we still have to show whether that involves the kind of self-model he describes and why it would give rise to an experience of being that self.

I can take seriously the argument that we might never be able to know whether a machine has an experience of its own, and we might have to live with that uncertainty however capable these systems become. That uncertainty allows for possibilities in both directions; it gives us no grounds to assume consciousness has arrived. I think anyone making that claim needs to explain how they’ve established it from the behaviour we can actually observe, because a fluent conversation is precisely what these systems have been trained to produce.
